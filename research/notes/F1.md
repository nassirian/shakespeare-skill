### Scope summary
- **P1**: 18 MFA-trained writers edited 1,057 paragraphs (avg 205 words) from Claude-3.5-Sonnet (368), GPT-4o (393), Llama-3.1-70B (296); 8,035 span edits (~8/paragraph). ~80% literary fiction, rest creative nonfiction (travel, food, personal essay, advice); prompts reverse-engineered from New Yorker, NYT (36 Hours, Cooking, Modern Love), Dear Sugar. Formative study: 8 writers, 200 paragraphs, ~1,600 edits, 50 initial categories collapsed to 7. English, 2024.
- **P2**: LAMP edits used to train Writing Quality Reward Models (WQRM); Writing Quality benchmark of 4,729 pairwise judgments (Art or Artifice 144, LAMP-test 1,206, Style Mimic 300, Synthetic Mirror 1,120, LM Arena 1,959). Expert study: 9 writers, 300 triplet rankings.
- **P3**: 12 New Yorker stories vs. GPT-3.5, GPT-4, Claude v1.3 versions (48 stories, ~expanded to within 200 words of the human story); 10 experts (8 shaped the rubric) applied 14 binary Torrance-derived tests. 2023 models.
- **P4**: 10,272 Books3 human stories (avg ~6,400 words) vs. one story each from Claude Sonnet 4.6, DeepSeek V3.2, Gemini 3 Flash, GPT-5.4, Kimi K2.5 for the same reverse-engineered prompt (61,608 stories). 304 LLM-extracted narrative features (257 after removing 47 style features), 10 NarraBench dimensions. Extractor–human κ 0.84 (human–human 0.74).
- **P5**: 19-expert survey → taxonomy; 3 Upwork professional copy-editors annotated spans on 150 news articles (human, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, GPT-4o, o1-pro, "humanized" versions; avg 871 words, from Russell et al. 2025) and 100 MS MARCO QA answers (Llama-4 Scout, OLMo-2, Mistral-7B, GPT-4o, Gemma-2; avg 55 words).
- **P6**: POS n-gram (n=4–8) templates; summarization (Rotten Tomatoes, Cochrane, CNN/DM), open generation (OLMo-7B, Cosmopedia), book summarization (BooookScore); models OLMo-7B, Mistral-7B, Llama-2/3, Alpaca, GPT-4o, Claude, ChatGPT, GPT-4, Mixtral.

## Pattern entries

### A. Sentence- and phrase-level (P1, P2, P3, P6)

#### 1. Clichés and stock images
- **What the paper found**: P1 category "Cliché" = 17% of all 8,035 expert edits (Fig. 5a; 4th most common). Examples flagged: "the numbers glaring back at me like an unsolvable riddle" → writer edit "The numbers stared back"; stock opener "As Sarah stepped off the bus, the scent of pine and damp earth enveloped her" (Table 5); "her mind a whirlpool of restless thoughts"; "reflect on the kaleidoscope of experiences you've had" (Table 13). Shared stock phrases across all three model families: "air was thick," "hung in the air," "eyes darting," "a sense of unease," "in the pit of [the stomach]" (§5.3). Writer W3: "the massive amount of cliché, histrionic descriptions, and direct exposition of intended meanings" (§7.1). P3: "Originality in Thought" ("Is the story an original piece of writing without any cliches?") passed by New Yorker 91.7% vs. GPT-3.5 2.8%, GPT-4 44.4%, Claude 1.3 19.4%; expert on GPT-3.5: "The piece relies on cliched turns of phrase to express actions and thoughts" (Table 6). P2 Appendix flags "a delicate bloom unfurling in the wake of a harsh winter."
- **Scope**: P1 Claude-3.5/GPT-4o/Llama-3.1-70B, creative fiction/nonfiction, 2024; P3 2023 models, short fiction.
- **Actionable rule**: Scan for and replace stock sensory/atmospheric phrases ("the air was thick with," "hung in the air," "eyes darting," "a knot/pit in the stomach," "sense of unease," "X enveloped her"). When a simile explains a plain action ("glaring back at me like an unsolvable riddle"), cut the simile and keep the plain verb ("stared back"). Prefer a concrete, observed detail over a ready-made image.
- **Caveats**: P1 §7.2: LLM rewriters "sometimes replace clichés with other clichés" — swapping one stock phrase for another does not fix it. Amateur human writers also use clichés (P3 §7.1). Expert agreement on category labels is low (categorical precision 0.23), so "cliché vs. purple prose vs. awkward" boundaries blur.
- **Strength**: strong (large expert-annotated corpus + replicated in P3 with different models/experts).

#### 2. Unnecessary / redundant exposition ("fluff")
- **What the paper found**: P1 "Unnecessary/Redundant Exposition" = 18% of edits; defined as restating what is obvious or already implied, or details that do little work; writers' own term "fluff." Example: a sentence saying the mountain "cast a long shadow" flagged as stating the obvious; a sentence about the expressway's concrete "behemoth" restating what the scene already conveys. Llama-3.1-70B received the most edits in this category. Deletions are 18% of all edits.
- **Scope**: P1 as above.
- **Actionable rule**: After drafting, delete any sentence that restates what the previous sentence showed or what the reader can infer. Delete summary sentences that explain the significance of a just-shown detail.
- **Caveats**: Some writers in P1 favored deletion more than others (writer style varies; W3 9.4 edits/para vs. W12/W16 ~6).
- **Strength**: strong.

#### 3. Explaining subtext / telling emotions and meanings
- **What the paper found**: P3 "Rhetorical Complexity" ("Does the story operate at multiple 'levels' of meaning (surface and subtext)?") pass rates: New Yorker 88.9% vs. GPT-3.5 2.8%, GPT-4 11.1%, Claude 5.6% — among the largest gaps. Expert quotes: E1 "AI-written dialogues disappointingly lacking in subtext"; E1 dialogue makes "hackish sense as clumsy exposition directed at the reader"; E2 "the AI seemed entirely unable to use implication or subtext"; AI characters "frequently over-explain situations and emotions." Table 7 failing traits for Scene vs Exposition: "Overreliance on summary leaves the narrative ungrounded" (pass: NY 91.7%, GPT-3.5 8.3%, GPT-4 50%, Claude 58.3%). P2 names "lack of subtext" as a target idiosyncrasy and says LLMs struggle with "revealing subtext." P1 W3: "direct exposition of intended meanings."
- **Scope**: P3 2023 models; P2 2024–25 models.
- **Actionable rule**: Do not have narrators or characters state what a scene means or what someone feels when action, dialogue, or detail already implies it. In dialogue, characters should not explain the situation to each other for the reader's benefit. Leave at least one meaning implied.
- **Caveats**: See P4 entry #15 — P4 finds humans use *explicit* emotion labels more often (29% vs. 8%) while AI uses embodied sensation; "over-explaining" in AI is more about themes and lessons than one-word emotion names.
- **Strength**: strong (consistent across P1, P2, P3, P4).

#### 4. Purple prose / ornamental figurative language
- **What the paper found**: P1 category "Purple Prose": overly ornate writing with sprawling sentences and excess adjectives, adverbs, metaphors that draw attention to style. Example: "The sobs emerged from this deep well of unspoken expectations." Example rewrites: "confusion clouded his understanding like the haze hanging over the park" → "he remained confused"; "It's the democratization of ingredients that sets the bánh mì apart" → "It's the variety of ingredients…" (Table 13). GPT-4o received the most purple-prose edits. P3: "Language Proficiency & Literary Devices" failing trait: "Attempts at figurative language come across as overwrought, confusing, nonsensical"; GPT-4 described as equating good writing with lofty, superficial figurative language; four of the experts report metaphors that are "either incoherent or overly simple." P2 Table 6 rationale: "avoiding the overwrought metaphors."
- **Actionable rule**: Cap figurative language: at most one metaphor per passage, and only if it carries information plain words can't. Replace abstract-noun inflation ("the democratization of ingredients") with the plain word ("variety"). Cut metaphors whose two halves don't cohere.
- **Caveats**: Exact share of edits not reported (below 17%). Literary humans also use metaphor heavily; P3 New Yorker stories pass literary-devices at 88.9% — the issue is ill-fitting, decorative figuration, not figuration per se.
- **Strength**: strong.

#### 5. Awkward word choice: hedge verbs ("seemed to"), misfit vocabulary, passive voice
- **What the paper found**: P1 "Awkward Word Choice and Phrasing" = 28% of edits — the single most common category. Includes misused/imprecise words, unclear pronoun references, overused passive voice. Writer W1: "seem to (verb)" constructions are "inelegant"; "'amplified' is better than 'seemed to amplify'"; unless there is real doubt, "just use the verb without 'seem'." Example: "the sky seemed to hover" → "hovered." Writers in §7.1 cite "misfit vocabulary" and "mechanical tone."
- **Actionable rule**: Replace "seemed to X / appeared to X / began to X" with the verb itself unless doubt or inception is the point. Check each pronoun has one clear antecedent. Prefer active voice. Replace words that are slightly off-register for the context.
- **Caveats**: Broad, heterogeneous category; boundary with purple prose and cliché is fuzzy.
- **Strength**: strong.

#### 6. Poor sentence structure: run-ons and trailing participial/simile tails
- **What the paper found**: P1 "Poor Sentence Structure" = 20% of edits (2nd most common): convoluted or run-on sentences, disjointed transitions; "Splitting long thoughts often helps." Example: "As the night wore on, Z.'s laughter grew louder, his words slurring together like a sloppy melody" (opener clause + main clause + participial tail + simile). §7.3 recommends splitting long sentences. P3 E4: "AI writing on the other hand demonstrated unusual/inconsistent syntax."
- **Actionable rule**: Watch the shape "As [time clause], [main clause], [noun] [verb-ing] like [simile]." Split into two sentences or cut the trailing participial/simile tail. Vary openings rather than leading with "As…" subordinate clauses.
- **Caveats**: The specific "As X, Y, Z-ing like W" shape is inferred from the paper's examples, not counted by the paper.
- **Strength**: strong for the category; moderate for the specific shape.

#### 7. Tense inconsistency and "began to" inceptives
- **What the paper found**: P1 category 7: unintended tense shifts. Example: "The first snowflakes began to drift" → "drifted down from the heavens."
- **Actionable rule**: Keep tense consistent; drop "began to/started to" unless the start is the point.
- **Caveats**: Share not reported; presumably small.
- **Strength**: moderate.

#### 8. Lack of specificity (broad generalizations)
- **What the paper found**: P1 "Lack of Specificity and Detail": reliance on broad generalizations that keep readers from visualizing; these edits usually make the text *longer* (insertions = 8% of edits). Example flagged: "Pain was Dr. Arthur Steiger's forte." §7.2: LLM rewriters "struggled with adding specific detail." P2 §7: "in the absence of original good-quality content, all LLMs are poor writers"; with ~30-word prompts, award-winning authors scored median 8.9 on WQRM vs. LLMs 4.8–6.6; with detailed content prompts LLMs reached ~7.0 vs. MFA students 8.6.
- **Actionable rule**: Replace generalizations with a particular instance (a name, number, object, action). When revising AI drafts, the human must supply specifics the model cannot invent; do not ask the model to "add detail" and accept generic additions.
- **Caveats**: Specificity requires facts; in nonfiction, invented specifics are a factuality risk.
- **Strength**: strong (P1 + P2 content experiment).

#### 9. Recurring lexical/phrasal templates: "a sense of," "a mix of X and Y," "the weight of," "unspoken"
- **What the paper found**: P1 §5.3/Table 8: of the 50 most common POS templates in LLM paragraphs, 15 occur far less in the human seed paragraphs. Examples: DT NN IN NN CC — "a mix of pride and," "a sense of protection and"; NN IN NN CC NN — "mix of relief and gratitude"; DT JJ NN IN PRP$ — "the subtle shift in their"; DT NN IN JJ NN — "a state of constant unease"; IN DT NN IN NN CC — "with a mix of wariness and." "unspoken" appears in ~15% of LLM responses; "weight of," "sense of," "mix of" rare or absent in human seed text. Same phrases appear across all three model families (authors suggest shared instruction-tuning or synthetic data). P3 E3: "Some words or phrases tended to be used again and again over multiple stories"; E1: "repeated pet phrases or words."
- **Actionable rule**: Flag and mostly cut: "a sense of [abstract noun]," "a mix of [emotion] and [emotion]," "the weight of [abstract noun]," "a state of [adj] [noun]," "the subtle shift in," "unspoken [anything]." Name the one emotion that matters, or show it, instead of blending two abstractions.
- **Caveats**: These are fiction-domain counts against 2024 models; each phrase alone is normal English — the signal is frequency.
- **Strength**: moderate–strong.

#### 10. Repeated syntactic templates (POS n-grams) in general
- **What the paper found**: P6: on Rotten Tomatoes summaries ~95% of model outputs contain a template vs. ~38% for human references (Table 3: reference 46.4%, inputs 29.3%); CNN/DM: references 36.0% vs. models 83.2% (Llama-3-70B) to 97.4% (Llama-2-13B); Cochrane: references 83.3% vs. models 67.0–99.7% (formulaic genre). OLMo-7B open-ended greedy: 100% templated. Examples: "RB , DT NN VBD" → "Fiercely , the beast breathed, Slowly , the caterpillar inched"; DT JJ NN IN DT JJ NN → "a romantic comedy about a corporate executive." 75–76% of OLMo templates appear in pre-training data (vs. 34% of random sequences); templates are learned early in pre-training and are "not overwritten during fine-tuning or alignment." Larger models do not template less. POS-level "style memorization" 6.4% vs. 5.3% exact-text.
- **Actionable rule**: Vary sentence skeletons, not just words: don't open consecutive sentences with "Adverb, the noun verbed"; avoid repeating the same "the [adj] [noun] of the [adj] [noun]" frame. Check a paragraph for sentences with identical grammatical shape.
- **Caveats**: Human genres can be highly templated (Cochrane 83%). Sampling lowers template rate (71–77% vs. 100% greedy for OLMo) but not in summarization (~96–98%). Metric is about rate per text, not a per-sentence rule.
- **Strength**: strong (peer-reviewed EMNLP, multiple models/tasks), but evidence is mostly summarization.

### B. Narrative / discourse level (P3, P4)

#### 11. Over-explicit themes and moralizing; narrator states the lesson
- **What the paper found**: P4 Table 16: "Thematic Explicitness & Moralizing" (1–5) human 3.28 vs. AI 3.94; "Narratorial Thematic Commentary → yes" 52% vs. 77%; "Moral/Philosophical Weighting" 3.26 vs. 3.68; "Thematic Unity" 4.41 vs. 4.74; "Dialogue Function → philosophical debate" 34% vs. 59%. Example given: in a grief arc, AI typically ends with the narrator stating the lesson learned. P3 Table 7 failing endings: "overly moralistic conclusions"; §7.1 experts flag "overly moralizing voice" and "predictable topic and summary sentences."
- **Scope**: P4 2026 frontier models (Claude Sonnet 4.6, GPT-5.4, Gemini 3 Flash, DeepSeek V3.2, Kimi K2.5), ~5,000-word fiction vs. Books3.
- **Actionable rule**: Cut sentences in which the narrator names the theme or lesson. Don't let dialogue turn into a debate about ideas; keep it about the characters' concrete wants. Allow loose ends that don't serve the theme.
- **Caveats**: Human stories also explain themes 52% of the time — the issue is degree and default, not presence.
- **Strength**: strong (very large corpus, recent models).

#### 12. Tidy, earned-by-epiphany endings; resolution through inner acceptance
- **What the paper found**: P4: "Resolution Mode → internal understanding" 27% (human) vs. 47% (AI); "Agency in Resolution → protagonist choice" 46% vs. 69%; humans "more comfortable with ambiguous endings." Gemini: tidiest endings, extended denouements; Claude favors epilogues/flash-forwards and quiet endings. P3 "Narrative Ending" pass: NY 91.7% vs. GPT-3.5 8.3%, GPT-4 19.4%, Claude 33.3%; failing traits "Abrupt or unearned resolutions, overly moralistic conclusions, unrealistic happy endings"; E5: "The AI rarely knew how to end a story"; E4: AI "forestalling of the ending… the falling action falls…and falls."
- **Actionable rule**: End on an action, image, or unresolved tension rather than a realization or acceptance. Cut epilogues and extended denouements. Let external events, not only the protagonist's choice, sometimes determine outcomes. Stop soon after the climax.
- **Caveats**: P3 Western "highbrow" literary bias acknowledged by authors (§7.4).
- **Strength**: strong.

#### 13. Single-track, linear, tightly causal plots
- **What the paper found**: P4: "Subplot Integration → no subplots" 57% vs. 79% (AI); "thematically parallel subplots" 42% vs. 21%; "Causal Chain Continuity" 3.92 vs. 4.20; "Chronological Discontinuity" 2.40 vs. 2.12; "Nonlinear Framing for Delayed Disclosure" 1.96 vs. 1.68; "Anachrony Intensity" 2.58 vs. 2.31; "Depth of Recontextualization After Surprise" 3.28 vs. 2.95; human fingerprint "Overall revelation pacing → back-loaded." Example: a human mystery "might open at the funeral and spiral backward through decades." P3 "Structural Flexibility" ("turns that are both surprising and appropriate") NY 88.9% vs. 11.1/19.4/30.6%; "Originality in Form" NY 63.9% vs. 2.8/8.3/0.0%.
- **Actionable rule**: In longer fiction, consider a parallel subplot that echoes the theme, non-chronological ordering, and withholding a key fact so a late reveal reframes earlier scenes. Allow some events that are not strictly caused by the previous one.
- **Caveats**: Feature-level, not sentence-level; mostly for long fiction.
- **Strength**: strong.

#### 14. Morally clean protagonists; flat, stereotyped secondary characters
- **What the paper found**: P4: "Moral Polarity → ambivalent/mixed" 59% (human) vs. 38% (AI). P3 "Character Development" NY 61.1% vs. 8.3/16.7/16.7%; "Perspective & Voice Flexibility" failing trait: "Narrator's perspective dominates, and the other characters are not portrayed convincingly as full human beings"; GPT-4 story characters "flattened by stereotype"; E3: characters appear and disappear without impact.
- **Actionable rule**: Give protagonists choices that are morally mixed; give secondary characters wants that conflict with the narrator's; avoid identity-as-trait characterization.
- **Strength**: strong.

#### 15. Embodied-emotion clichés and smell-heavy sensory boilerplate
- **What the paper found**: P4: "Emotional Expression → embodied" (bodily sensation/metaphor) 38% (human) vs. 81% (AI) — the largest gap in the core table; "Emotional Expression → explicit labels" 29% (human) vs. 8% (AI); "Sensory Modalities → olfactory" 57% vs. 82%; "Sensory Density" 3.66 vs. 3.93; "Depth of Interior Access" 3.67 vs. 3.93; "Setting as Psychological Mirror" 3.58 vs. 4.07; "Environmental & Ecological Emphasis" 2.83 vs. 3.21. P1's stock phrases ("in the pit of [stomach]," "scent of pine and damp earth enveloped her") are instances.
- **Actionable rule**: Don't default to rendering every emotion as a body sensation (tight chest, knot in stomach, breath catching). Sometimes simply name the emotion plainly, or show it through behavior or dialogue. Limit smell-led scene openings. Don't make weather/setting mirror the character's mood.
- **Caveats**: Counter-intuitive versus "show don't tell" advice — P4 shows that over-applied "show" (embodiment) is itself an AI tell, and plain labels are more human. DeepSeek's fingerprint is "behavioral cues" for emotion, so behavior-only rendering can also be a model default.
- **Strength**: strong.

#### 16. Vague literary allusion instead of named references
- **What the paper found**: P4: "Reference Explicitness → implicit echoes" 50% (human) vs. 72% (AI); "Intertextual Strategy → explicit named reference" 47% vs. 24%; "balanced mix" 37% vs. 16%. Claude especially "reverent/continuist" toward literary tradition (62% vs. 39–56%).
- **Actionable rule**: If referring to another work, name it (title, author, song) rather than gesturing at "an old story" or "the poets."
- **Strength**: moderate–strong.

#### 17. No fourth wall, no reader address; external-description character intros
- **What the paper found**: P4: "Fourth-Wall Permeability" 0.67 (human) vs. 0.39; "Direct Reader Address" 0.28 vs. 0.07; "Character Introduction → external description" 30% vs. 52%; human fingerprint "Character introduction → in-dialogue" (highest uniqueness 21.4); "Dialogue-to-Narration Proportion" human 2.95 vs. 2.70; "Opening Spatial Grounding" human 2.12 vs. 2.33 (AI grounds the opening in place more).
- **Actionable rule**: Introduce characters through what they say or do, not a physical description paragraph. Use more dialogue. Don't always open by establishing the setting. Direct reader address is acceptable where the voice supports it.
- **Strength**: moderate–strong.

#### 18. Pacing: rushed time after the first scene; summary over scene
- **What the paper found**: P3 "Narrative Pacing" NY 94.4% vs. 8.3/52.8/61.1%; failing trait "Abrupt, jarring shifts in time or scene"; E2: a model would "rapidly accelerate through time after the first scene." "Scene vs Exposition" failing: "Overreliance on summary." P3 also: LLMs wrote stories 20–50% shorter than requested.
- **Actionable rule**: Dramatize key moments as scenes; don't summarize the middle of a story to reach the end.
- **Strength**: moderate (n=48 stories, 2023 models).

#### 19. Per-model fingerprints (P4 Table 17; useful to know which defaults to counter)
- **Claude (Sonnet 4.6)**: low event escalation ("Strength of event escalation" uniq. 22.4), low event-type diversity, epilogue/flash-forward endings, no dreams/visions, uncanny/haunted settings, most uniform voice, quiet endings, reverent to literary tradition.
- **GPT (5.4)**: gossip/rumor as plot mechanism (64% vs. 44–55%), distant retrospective narrator ("years later"), subverting expectations (41% vs. 27–36%), no habitual/iterative narration, partial/ambiguous reconciliations.
- **Gemini (3 Flash)**: tidiest endings, extended denouements, bleak/oppressive settings (88%), protagonist's social world expands, mostly direct speech, siege/ordeal schema, named characters, frequent flashbacks, external character description.
- **DeepSeek (V3.2)**: visible narrator, emotion via behavioral cues, atmosphere over plot, evenly interleaved backstory, embedded storytelling scenes, front-loads crucial context.
- **Kimi (K2.5)**: fewest fingerprints; in-action character introductions, in medias res; generic center of the AI distribution.
- **Human fingerprint**: in-dialogue character introductions, single focal character, no direct address (in some), back-loaded revelation, crossover genre.
- **Actionable rule**: When revising Claude-style drafts, raise stakes and escalate events; for GPT-style, drop the "years later, I still remember" retrospective frame and rumor-driven plots; for Gemini-style, cut the denouement and lighten relentless bleakness.
- **Caveats**: Fingerprints are relative to the other four models, single model versions, 2026.
- **Strength**: moderate (large n, but model-version-specific).

#### 20. AI stories cluster; human stories are rarer
- **What the paper found**: P4: mean rarity percentile human 0.71 vs. AI 0.49; 24.7% of human stories in the top-10% rarest vs. 7.1% of AI. AI stories occupy a shared region of narrative space. P3: "Originality in Theme & Content" NY 75% vs. 0/19.4/11.1%; Claude: "While the piece avoids overused expressions, its ideas and themes are hackneyed."
- **Actionable rule**: Avoiding phrase-level clichés is not enough; ask whether the premise, structure, and resolution are the first ones that come to mind, and choose a less expected (still fitting) option.
- **Strength**: strong.

### C. Expository / informational text (P5)

#### 21. Low information density and irrelevance (biggest drivers of "slop")
- **What the paper found**: P5 logistic regression: all 7 annotated codes positively predict a "slop" label; largest coefficients Relevance (β̂ = 0.06), Density (0.05), Tone (0.05). News: Coherence, Tone, Density, Relevance, Bias significant. QA: Factuality and structural issues strongest. Span counts correlate with slop judgments ρ = 0.70 (news), 0.51 (QA). Expert survey code frequencies (of 19): Relevance 9, Factuality 7, Repetition 7, Coherence 6, Density 5, Verbosity 5, Fluency 4, Tone 3, Bias 2, Templatedness 2, Word Complexity 1. Relevance example (all annotators): "During the Roman Empire, physicians developed techniques to repair injured gladiators and soldiers" (background tangent). Guideline example for relevance: a marathon question answered with generic exercise benefits. Density: "verbose text that conveys little information, with generic or filler content." ~34% of news articles labeled slop by annotators.
- **Scope**: news (human, Claude-3.5-Sonnet, GPT-4o, o1-pro, humanized) and QA; English.
- **Actionable rule**: Every paragraph must answer the actual question/task; cut historical or general background the reader didn't ask for. Measure each sentence by whether it adds a new fact or claim; cut those that don't.
- **Caveats**: No per-source breakdown (human vs. model) was found in the retrieved text; humans write slop too (Table 10: "The classic highschool transitional words that students just learning to write essays use, without variance"). Low binary agreement (κ −0.15 to 0.29).
- **Strength**: moderate.

#### 22. Tone mismatch / generic voice; unnatural-but-correct phrasing; needless complexity; templated sentences
- **What the paper found**: P5 Appendix F guideline definitions with examples: Tone — generic voice or mismatched formality: "The aforementioned destination offers numerous recreational activities" in a personal travel blog; Fluency/"Language Naturalness" — technically correct but unnatural: "The earthen area that formerly held the puddle was now dry"; Word Complexity — jargon/rare vocabulary unsuited to audience; Templatedness — repeated formulaic frames like "Dr. X, a researcher at Y"; Repetition — reuse of words/phrases ("The project was a success."); Bias — one-sided or too "objective" when a view is needed: "The economic policy changes of 2023 were universally beneficial." Tone annotated span: "The very power of the word ['witch'] lies in its imprecision." Factuality span: "Climate change is like adding steroids to our weather," (real scientist, misattributed quote).
- **Actionable rule**: Match register to venue (no "aforementioned"/"numerous" in casual prose). Prefer the plain noun over periphrasis ("the dry patch where the puddle was"). Don't repeat the "Name, a [role] at [institution], said" frame for every source. Don't invent or misattribute quotes; verify attributions. Take a position where the genre calls for one.
- **Caveats**: Tone agreement among copy-editors was low (AC1 0.20).
- **Strength**: moderate.

### D. Process-level findings (P1, P2)

#### 23. Edit operations mix and where effort goes
- **What the paper found**: P1: replacements 74%, deletions 18%, insertions 8%; ~70% of non-deletion edits preserved meaning (BERTScore ≥0.6; authors call the threshold "fuzzy/arbitrary"). Lower-rated paragraphs got more edits (rated 2 → 10.2 edits; rated 10 → 2.4; r = −0.31). No significant quality difference across the three models; nonfiction ~5.2 vs. fiction 4.5. P2: median WQRM score rose ~0.2 per reapplied expert edit.
- **Actionable rule**: Revision of AI text is mostly local replacement and cutting; expect ~8 edits per 200 words; meaning can be preserved while fixing style.
- **Strength**: strong.

#### 24. Detect → rewrite pipeline helps but doesn't reach experts
- **What the paper found**: P1 preference study (200 triplets, 600 annotations): writer-edited ranked first 65% of the time (avg rank ~1.5); LLM-edited (detect + category-specific few-shot rewrite) avg 1.99; unedited LLM text last 60% (avg 2.51); Kendall's W 0.505. P2 generate→edit (N=20)→rank-by-WQRM: best edit avg rank 1.58 vs. random edit 2.09 vs. draft 2.26 (9 experts). P2 authors: output "no where near expert quality writing."
- **Actionable rule**: A category-by-category self-edit pass (cliché, exposition, purple prose, structure, specificity, word choice, tense) measurably improves text; generating several candidate revisions and choosing the best helps further.
- **Strength**: strong.

## 1. Human-text features (what human writing does that LLM writing lacks)
- Implication and subtext; dialogue that doesn't explain itself (P3 Rhetorical Complexity 88.9% vs ≤11.1%).
- Earned, natural endings that stop without moralizing (P3); ambiguous endings, outcomes driven by external events (P4: resolution via internal understanding only 27%).
- Morally ambivalent protagonists (59% vs. 38%) and fully realized secondary characters (P3, P4).
- Parallel/thematic subplots (42% vs. 21%), time jumps, nonlinear framing, back-loaded revelations that reframe earlier scenes (P4).
- Named, explicit references to specific works/authors (47% vs. 24%) (P4).
- Plain explicit emotion labels (29% vs. 8%) instead of constant bodily sensation (P4).
- Fourth-wall permeability and direct reader address (P4); character introductions through dialogue (P4 human fingerprint).
- More dialogue relative to narration (P4).
- Greater variety: human stories are rarer in narrative space (rarity 0.71 vs 0.49) (P4); fewer repeated POS templates (P6: e.g., CNN/DM references 36% vs. models 83–97%).
- Specific content: award-winning authors bring original content; with the same detailed content, MFA students still out-score LLMs (8.6 vs ~7.0) (P2).
- Precise single verbs instead of "seemed to/began to" (P1).
- Original form and theme (P3 Originality in Form NY 63.9% vs ≤8.3%).

## 2. Things that do NOT work or backfire
- **LLM rewriting swaps clichés for clichés** and struggles to add specific detail (P1 §7.2).
- **Surface-level editing / humanizing leaves narrative signal**: LAMP span-level rewriting of 278 Gemini stories lowered narrative-feature detection only from 95.5 to 93.9 macro-F1; narrative features are "largely orthogonal" to surface artifacts; changing them needs "significant structural rewrites" (P4). P2 also cites Russell et al. 2025 that paraphrasing/humanization does not remove AI signatures.
- **Avoiding overused expressions is not enough**: Claude 1.3 stories avoided clichéd phrases but themes were "hackneyed" (P3 Table 6).
- **Over-applied "show, don't tell"** (embodied emotion, sensory density, setting-as-mirror) is itself an AI pattern (P4).
- **LLMs as judges of writing quality are unreliable**: TTCW κ ≈ 0 (GPT-3.5 0.016, GPT-4 0.035, Claude −0.006) (P3); zero-shot pairwise quality judging near chance (GPT-4o 40.9%, o1 51.7%, R1 46.0%, o3 64.3%) and LLMs preferred their own paragraph over Annie Ernaux's (P2 Table 3); zero-shot slop judges κ ≈ 0, flag 3–8% vs. humans 34% (P5); span detectors stay below expert agreement (P1 0.38–0.46 vs 0.57). Implication: a model's self-assessment "this reads human" is weak evidence.
- **More few-shot examples** (>5, or 2→25) give little gain for detection/judging (P1, P2, P3).
- **Bigger models don't template less** (P6); **temperature/sampling** reduces templates in open generation but not in summarization (P6); alignment doesn't remove templates (P6).
- **Generic reward models and AI detectors** don't measure quality (Skywork 60.5%, Pangram 65.0% on WQ, near random on human-human pairs) (P2).
- **Simple automatic metrics** (entropy, idea density, readability, template rates) in a linear model reach only AUPRC ~0.52–0.55 for slop (P5).

## 3. Statistical / theory content translated to writing behavior
- P6: templates come from pre-training data and are the model's high-frequency syntactic defaults → vary sentence frames deliberately; a sentence skeleton that "comes easily" is likely the common one. Synonym-swapping inside the same frame preserves the template (P6 shows "style memorization" via synonym swaps), so restructure the sentence, don't just change words.
- P5 metrics (token entropy, idea density, templates-per-token, Gunning-Fog) → aim for more propositions per sentence and plainer vocabulary, but these metrics alone poorly predict human slop judgments.

## 5-line summary
1. Expert edits of AI prose (P1, 8,035 edits) concentrate in awkward word choice (28%), poor sentence structure (20%), redundant exposition (18%) and clichés (17%), with shared phrases across model families ("unspoken" in ~15% of outputs; "a sense of," "a mix of X and Y," "the weight of," "hung in the air").
2. At narrative level (P3, P4), AI fiction states its themes and lessons (77% vs 52%), resolves through inner acceptance and protagonist choice, avoids subplots and nonlinear time, has morally clean protagonists, and lacks subtext (Rhetorical Complexity 88.9% human vs ≤11.1% AI).
3. Over-applied "show" is a tell: AI renders emotion through bodily sensation in 81% of stories vs 38% human, leads with smell (82% vs 57%), and uses vague allusions; humans more often name emotions plainly (29% vs 8%) and name the works they cite.
4. Surface rewriting doesn't remove AI signals: LLM editors swap one cliché for another, POS templates survive synonym swaps and alignment (P6), and LAMP-style span edits leave narrative detection near 94 F1 (P4). Fixing it takes structural change and real, specific content (P2).
5. In expository text, readers judge "slop" mainly by relevance, density and tone (P5). LLM judges are near chance at assessing quality or slop (P2, P3, P5), so a model's own "reads human" verdict should not be trusted.
