## A. Grammatical and rhetorical patterns

### 1. Overuse of present-participial clauses
- **What the paper found:** [P1] GPT-4o uses present participial clauses at **5.3× the human rate** (Cohen's d = 1.38). Instruction-tuned models in general sit at **2–5×**. Example from the paper (GPT-4o): *"Bryan, leaning on his agility, dances around the ring, evading Show's heavy blows."* It is the top feature by random-forest importance (Fig. 3).
- **Scope:** GPT-4o, GPT-4o Mini, and Llama 3 8B/70B in base and Instruct versions. The task was to continue a 500-word human chunk. Human corpus HAP-E: 12k texts across academic, news, fiction, spoken, blogs, and TV/movie scripts, with COCA-based CAP for replication. English, 2024.
- **Actionable rule:** Limit sentences to at most one -ing participle clause. Turn "X, doing Y, does Z" into two finite clauses or a subordinate clause ("Bryan relied on his agility. He danced around the ring while Show swung"). Cut trailing ", [verb]-ing X" tails.
- **Caveats:** Humans use these clauses too, especially in fiction. The problem is density, not presence. Base Llama models were close to human rates, which points to instruction tuning as the cause.
- **Strength:** Strong. Peer reviewed (PNAS), large paired corpus, replicated on a second corpus.

### 2. Nominalization and noun-heavy, "informationally dense" style
- **What the paper found:** [P1] Nominalizations run at **2.1× in GPT-4o** (d = 1.23) and **1.5–2×** in instruction-tuned models generally. Example (Llama 3 70B Instruct, four nominalizations): *"These schemes can help to reduce deforestation, habitat destruction, and pollution…"* The authors describe instruction-tuned output as "informationally dense" and "noun-heavy."
- **Scope:** Same as P1 above.
- **Actionable rule:** Convert -tion/-ment/-ness nouns back into verbs with a named actor ("forests are cut down less," not "reduction of deforestation"). Prefer clause-based explanation over packed noun phrases, especially outside academic registers.
- **Caveats:** Academic prose is nominal by nature. Match the genre's norm rather than minimizing nominals everywhere.
- **Strength:** Strong.

### 3. Phrasal coordination and list stacking
- **What the paper found:** [P1] Phrasal coordination (X and Y, noun-and-noun pairs) runs at **1.9× in GPT-4o** (d = 0.81). [P7] identifies syntactic templates in LLM fiction such as *"a mix of pride and"* and *"a sense of protection and."*
- **Actionable rule:** Watch for paired and tripled nouns or adjectives ("pride and joy," "innovation and growth"). Keep one, or keep the pair only when both carry distinct information.
- **Caveats:** Coordination is normal English. Flag only the habit of pairing by default.
- **Strength:** Strong [P1]; moderate [P7].

### 4. "That"-clauses as subject
- **What the paper found:** [P1] **2.6× in GPT-4o** (d = 0.77).
- **Actionable rule:** Avoid openings like "That X is the case shows…" and similar extraposed or "It is clear that…" framings. State the claim directly.
- **Caveats:** The paper reports the feature only as a Biber category. Real examples are rare.
- **Strength:** Moderate. The effect is significant, but the feature is narrow.

### 5. Underuse of agentless passive
- **What the paper found:** [P1] GPT-4o uses the agentless passive at **about half the human rate**.
- **Actionable rule:** Do not purge passives mechanically. Human writers, especially in news and academic prose, use agentless passives where the agent is unknown or unimportant. An "always active voice" rule pushes text further from human norms.
- **Caveats:** This conflicts with [P7]'s writer-edit category "overuse of passive voice," which covers fiction. Treat the passive as genre-dependent.
- **Strength:** Strong.

### 6. Model-specific coordination and downtoner habits
- **What the paper found:** [P1] GPT-4o models avoid clausal coordination ("and" joining full clauses), while Llama 3 variants overuse it. GPT-4o uses downtoners ("barely," "nearly") more than humans, and Llama avoids them.
- **Actionable rule:** No universal rule. Vary sentence linkage, and do not let every sentence rely on the same kind of link.
- **Strength:** Strong, but the effects are model-specific.

### 7. Genre misalignment: one register for everything
- **What the paper found:** [P1] Instruction-tuned models cannot mimic the register of their prompt text, so fiction, news, spoken, and script continuations all drift toward the same dense expository style. Larger models were not more human-like than smaller ones (70B ≈ 8B; GPT-4o ≈ Mini). Base models were near human rates.
- **Actionable rule:** Before drafting, name the genre and its conventions: spoken text needs contractions, fragments, and short clauses; news needs attribution and passives; fiction needs dialogue and concrete action. Then check the draft against them. Do not use one "polished" register for every task.
- **Strength:** Strong.

## B. Vocabulary ("excess" and "slop" words)

### 8. Literary-abstract vocabulary in GPT-4o and Llama-Instruct prose
- **What the paper found:** [P1] Table 1 gives rate ratios against humans.
  - GPT-4o: camaraderie 162×, tapestry 155×, intricate 119×, underscore 107×, unspoken 102×, amidst 100×, palpable 95×, solace 95×, fleeting 84×, unravel 83×.
  - GPT-4o Mini adds grapple 131×, ignite 122×, vibrant 92×, cacophony 89×.
  - Llama 3 70B Instruct: unease 63×, palpable 47×, continuation 29×, shoutout 28×, intricate 27×, pang 25×, camaraderie 24×, policymaker 24×, prioritize 24×, reminder 24×.
  - Llama 3 8B Instruct adds rut, waft, and grapple.
  - "tapestry" appears in **23%** of GPT-4o outputs and "amidst" in **27%**.
  - Base models show no such style words; their overused items are artifacts such as names.
- **Actionable rule:** Treat these as banned defaults: tapestry, camaraderie, intricate, underscore, palpable, amidst, solace, fleeting, unravel, grapple, ignite, vibrant, cacophony, unease, pang, waft. Use one only when it is the exact word and no plainer word works, and never more than once in a piece.
- **Caveats:** Several of these words are normal in fiction. The authors note they are unconventional in academic, news, and script registers.
- **Strength:** Strong.

### 9. Excess "style words" in scientific abstracts
- **What the paper found:** [P2]
  - Of 15.1M PubMed abstracts (2010–2024), 2024 shows **454 excess words**, against 190 at the Covid peak in 2021. Style words dominate: of 379 excess style words, **66% are verbs and 14% adjectives**. Covid-era excess words were content nouns.
  - Top ratios: **delves 28.0×, underscores 13.8×, showcasing 10.7×**.
  - Largest absolute gaps: **potential δ = 0.052, findings 0.041, crucial 0.037**.
  - High-frequency excess set: across, additionally, comprehensive, crucial, enhancing, exhibited, insights, notably, particularly, within.
  - Selected rare excess words from the full list of 291: delve/delves/delving, underscore(s/d/ing), showcase(s/d/ing), intricate/intricacies, meticulous(ly), pivotal, realm(s), nuanced, multifaceted, interplay, landscape-type verbs (shaping, fostering, harnessing, leveraging, bolstering, paving, unveiling, unlocking, illuminating, elucidating, encompassing, navigating-family), commendable, noteworthy, notable, invaluable, groundbreaking, transformative, unparalleled, burgeoning, seamless(ly), swift(ly), strategically, holds/hold [promise], poised, pressing, imperative, garnered, surpass(es/ing), streamline, revolutionize, uncharted, underexplored, interconnectedness, endeavors, avenue(s), akin, amidst, alongside, consequently, conversely, ultimately, initially, typically, predominantly.
  - At least **13.5%** of 2024 abstracts were LLM-processed (lower bound). Subgroups range from under 5% to over 40%, e.g. computation papers from China at 0.41.
- **Scope:** PubMed abstracts, English, 2024 versus a 2021–22 extrapolated baseline. Model-agnostic, though mostly ChatGPT-era.
- **Actionable rule:** Run a "style-verb" pass. Replace showcase/underscore/highlight/delve/foster/leverage/harness/bolster/pave/unveil/elucidate/encompass with the literal verb (show, find, use, test, increase) or drop the frame ("This study delves into X" becomes "We measured X"). Cut evaluative adjectives (crucial, pivotal, comprehensive, notable, remarkable, invaluable) unless a number backs them. Cut sentence-initial "Additionally," "Notably," "Consequently," and "Ultimately."
- **Caveats:** Every one of these words is legitimate English and occurs in human text. The signal is excess frequency. "potential," "findings," and "across" are hard to avoid in science, so cap their density rather than ban them. The paper notes that native speakers may already edit out the worst offenders, so the list drifts. Vocabulary shifted again after 2024 (see P12 and P10 on newer tells).
- **Strength:** Strong. Peer reviewed, very large corpus, conservative lower-bound method.

### 10. Excess evaluative adjectives in peer reviews and papers
- **What the paper found:**
  - [P6a] After ChatGPT, "commendable," "meticulous," and "intricate" became **9.8×, 34.7×, and 11.2×** more likely to occur in a sentence (ICLR 2024 reviews, Fig. 1). Estimated LLM-modified review text was 6.5–16.9% (ICLR 10.6%, EMNLP 16.9%). Use was higher near deadlines, among reviewers who did not respond to rebuttals, and in low-confidence reviews. Reviews with scholarly citations were less likely to be AI-modified. Higher AI share correlated with more homogeneous reviews in embedding space. Proofreading-only edits barely moved the estimate, while expanding bullet-point notes into a full review explains much of the signal (§4.5–4.6).
  - [P6b] The top LLM-associated words in arXiv abstracts were "realm, intricate, showcasing, pivotal." CS abstracts reached 17.5% LLM-modified sentences by Feb 2024, against 4.9% for math. Use was higher among prolific first authors (19.3% vs 15.6%), in crowded areas (22.2% vs 14.7%), and in shorter papers (17.7% vs 13.6%).
  - I could not retrieve the full adverb/verb/noun appendix lists of [P6a].
- **Actionable rule:** Delete praise adjectives (commendable, meticulous, intricate, innovative, notable) in evaluative writing. Replace them with a specific observation or citation. A human reviewer's texture comes from engaging with specifics: citations, replies to the author's argument, confident claims.
- **Key behavioural finding:** "Expand my notes into prose" is the use pattern that produces the signal. Light proofreading does not. Shakespeare should not inflate terse notes into fluent paragraphs. It should keep the density of the notes.
- **Strength:** Strong (ICML/COLM, large corpora). The word-level claims rest on figures and appendices.

### 11. Creative-writing "slop" words and n-grams
- **What the paper found:** [P3]
  - Overrepresentation ratio = f_LLM / f_human. The human baseline is wordfreq for single words and Reddit creative writing plus Project Gutenberg for n-grams. The LLM sample is 2,000 Reddit-prompt stories per model.
  - Table 1 (gemma-3-12b): **elara 85,513×, unsettlingly 3,833×, shimmered 2,882×, stammered 2,043×**. Trigrams: **"heart hammered ribs" 1,192×, "voice trembling slightly" 731×, "said voice devoid" 693×, "felt profound sense" 550×**.
  - Appendix G Table 4 lists the words most shared across **67 models**, as the % of models with the word in their top-120 overrepresented list: flickered 98.5, flicker 94.0, flickering 92.5, leaned 82.1, muttered 82.1, gaze 80.6, grinned 80.6, containment 77.6, gestured 77.6, addendum 74.6, murmured 73.1, nodded 73.1, glint 68.7, hesitated 68.7, whispered 68.7, blinked 64.2, hummed 64.2, faintly 62.7, leans 62.7, unreadable 62.7.
  - Table 5 lists shared trigrams (stop-words removed): "voice barely whisper" 68.7, "said voice low" 61.2, "air thick scent" 49.3, "took deep breath" 44.8, "smile playing lips" 43.3, "something else something" 37.3, "voice barely audible" 35.8, "could shake feeling" 31.3, "eyes never leaving" 29.9, "casting long shadows" 28.4, "something else entirely" 26.9, "heart pounding chest" 25.4, "one last time" 23.9, "spreading across face" 22.4, "air thick smell" 19.4, "could help feel" 19.4.
  - Default character names include "Elara" and "Kael." Other categories are sensory clichés ("voice barely above a whisper") and intensifiers ("a profound sense").
- **Scope:** 67 open and closed models, English creative writing, 2025.
- **Actionable rule:** In fiction or narrative, do not reach for stock micro-gestures (flicker, glint, gaze, nodded, leaned, murmured, grinned, hesitated, blinked) as filler beats. Replace them with action specific to this character and scene, or with nothing. Ban the listed stock phrases: "voice barely above a whisper," "the air thick with the scent of," "took a deep breath," "a smile playing on her lips," "eyes never leaving," "casting long shadows," "heart pounding in her chest," "couldn't shake the feeling," "something else entirely," "a profound sense of." Never use Elara, Kael, or similar default fantasy names.
- **Caveats:** "nodded," "leaned," and "whispered" are ordinary words. The tell is how often they appear as beat-filler. Ratios against wordfreq inflate rare words.
- **Strength:** Strong for existence and ranking (ICLR, 67 models). Exact ratios depend on the baseline.

### 12. Model-specific discourse markers
- **What the paper found:** [P4] TF-IDF logistic regression identifies the source model with 85.5% accuracy (chat APIs).
  - ChatGPT: "such as," "certainly," "overall"; often opens with "Certainly" or "Below is."
  - Claude: "here," "according to," "based on"; uses "the/and/to/of" far less than other models.
  - Llama-Instruct: "including," "such as." Gemma: "let me," "know if" (as in "let me know if…").
  - Llama-Instruct continuations often open with "Here is the continuation of the text…" [P1].
- **Actionable rule:** Delete assistant framing at the edges of the text: "Certainly," "Here is," "Below is," "Overall," "Let me know if," "I hope this helps." Do not open with a preamble announcing the content, and do not close with an offer. Thin out "such as"/"including" lists.
- **Strength:** Strong (ICML).

## C. Constructions and rhetorical moves

### 13. "Not X, but Y" / "It's not X, it's Y" (negative parallelism)
- **What the paper found:**
  - [P3] The construction is "**6.3× more prevalent than human writing in some models**" (Fig. 7, occurrences per 1k chars). It had to be suppressed with regex because token bans cannot catch it. Appendix L gives four regex families:
    1. A negation ("not just/only/merely/because" or an "n't" word) followed by a sentence break and a new sentence starting "It/They/You…" ("It isn't X. It's Y.")
    2. A negated clause followed by a dash and a short word ("not X — Y")
    3. "no longer…" followed by a sentence break and "it/they/you is/are/was/were…"
    4. A negated clause followed by a comma, semicolon, or colon and "but" ("not X, but Y")
    - Pattern 1 begins `\b(?:\w+n(?:['’]t)|not\s+(?:just|only|merely|because))\s+`.
  - [P12, weak] The Atlantic/Barron's counted about 50 Fortune 500 filings using the construction in 2023 and over 200 in 2025. A Washington Post dataset found "not just X, but Y" variants in about 6% of ChatGPT messages.
- **Actionable rule:** Do not define something by first denying a straw alternative. Delete the negated half and state the positive claim. Apply this across sentence boundaries ("This isn't about money. It's about trust.") and to dash forms ("not a bug — a feature"). Allow at most one per long piece, and only when someone actually holds the denied view.
- **Caveats:** Real contrast with a view someone holds is legitimate rhetoric. P3 gives no human baseline number, and the P12 counts are journalistic.
- **Strength:** Moderate to strong. The ICLR paper quantifies it but gives limited detail.

### 14. Clichés, purple prose, and unnecessary exposition in literary writing
- **What the paper found:** [P7] 18 professional writers edited 1,057 LLM paragraphs (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, Llama 3.1 70B), making 8,035 edits. Share of edits by category: **awkward word choice/phrasing 28%, poor sentence structure 20%, unnecessary/redundant exposition 18%, cliché 17%**, followed by purple prose, lack of specificity, and tense inconsistency (percentages not retrieved). Edit operations were **74% replacements, 18% deletions, 8% insertions**. GPT-4o leans toward purple prose and Llama toward unnecessary exposition. Examples quoted:
  - cliché: *"a tapestry of modernity threaded with the hum of traffic"*; *"settled over her like a heavy blanket"*
  - exposition: *"a concrete behemoth that cast long shadows over the desolate landscape"*
  - purple prose: *"leaving behind a residue of weary resilience and a few hopeful echoes yet unwilling to completely extinguish"*
  - a writer's note on hedging: *"'amplified' is better than 'seemed to amplify'."*
- **Actionable rule:**
  - Cut sentences that explain what a scene already showed: emotional labels after action, or a closing line stating the meaning.
  - Replace simile clichés ("like a heavy blanket") with a concrete detail from the scene.
  - Remove stacked abstract nouns ("residue of weary resilience").
  - Drop hedging verbs (seemed to, appeared to, began to) when the action simply happened.
  - Fix awkward collocations: when a phrase is "almost right," find the precise word.
- **Caveats:** The corpus is about 80% literary fiction. LLM detection of these spans reached about 0.44 precision, against 0.57 for expert agreement.
- **Strength:** Moderate to strong. Expert annotation, mid-size corpus. I believe it was published at CHI 2025.

### 15. Em-dash overuse
- **What the paper found:**
  - [P10] In medRxiv Discussion sections (N = 69,632), em-dash prevalence rose from **4.23% before ChatGPT to 11.58% after** (OR 2.96). By year it was 8.0% in 2024 and **20.3% in 2025**. Placebo splits showed no change. The author frames it as a population-level indicator, not a per-document test.
  - [P11, weak] In 12 models, em dashes persisted when the model was told to avoid markdown (headers, bullets, and bold disappeared), and explicit prohibitions failed in some models. Llama produced 0 per 1k words; GPT-4.1 produced 9.1 per 1k words even under suppression. The tendency exists in base models.
- **Actionable rule:** Limit em dashes to about one per several paragraphs. Replace most of them with a comma, colon, parentheses, or a full stop. Check em dashes after drafting, since prohibiting them up front is unreliable [P11].
- **Caveats:** Em dashes are normal in edited human prose (journalism, essays). Many humans use them heavily, so they are a weak signal for any single text. Of the two papers, P10 has the stronger design (preregistered) but is a preprint.
- **Strength:** Moderate (P10). Weak (P11).

### 16. Markdown and formatting fingerprints
- **What the paper found:** [P4] With all words replaced by "xxx," markdown alone identifies the model with **73.1%** accuracy (chat) and **77.7%** (instruct), against a 25% chance level; base models score 38.5%. ChatGPT bolds enumerated points and uses headers. Claude rarely uses bold or headers and prefers plain bullets. Gemini and Gemma overuse italics. Asking for plain text or 100 words only modestly lowers model identifiability (96.3 → 91.4 / 93.0).
- **Actionable rule:** Prose requests get prose: no headers, bold lead-ins, or bullet lists unless the medium calls for them. Never bold the first words of list items as pseudo-headers. Use italics only for titles and true emphasis.
- **Strength:** Strong.

### 17. Persistent semantic and organizational fingerprint
- **What the paper found:** [P4] The model stays identifiable after paraphrase (97.8 → 91.4), translation (91.8), and even summarization (58.1, against 20% chance). Shuffling the word order barely hurts (88.9), while shuffling letters does (about 39%). The fingerprint lies in word choice and content selection: ChatGPT is "detailed, descriptive, engaging," Claude "concise… key points," Gemini "direct openings," Grok "rich and comprehensive."
- **Actionable rule:** Surface synonym swaps are not enough. What to say, and in what order, carries the signal. Choose content as a human expert would: lead with the specific point, skip the comprehensive survey, leave out the default "considerations."
- **Strength:** Strong.

## D. Homogenization and voice

### 18. LLM rewriting compresses stylistic variance and erases identity markers
- **What the paper found:** [P5]
  - Across 3 studies, 7 datasets, and over 880k texts, LLM rewriting ("Rephrase the following text" or "Rewrite… using the best syntax and grammar") preserves meaning (87% of rewrites have embedding cosine > 0.95) but **cuts variance in writing complexity by 21–50%**.
    - Example: Reddit with Llama 3 Rephrase went from 0.0091 to 0.0045 (F = 19.4, p < .001).
    - Example: Congress with Gemini Rephrase went from 0.0197 to 0.0083.
    - The composite combines Simpson/Shannon lexical diversity, type-token ratio, hapax legomena, and dependency length.
  - Trait classifiers lose accuracy after rewrites, especially for age and gender, because of "the selective erosion of well-established linguistic markers rather than a uniform loss."
    - The gender–negative-emotion-word association was "largely erased" (t = −3.30 → −1.20).
    - The age–future-focus association weakened (r −0.09 → −0.037).
    - Extraversion–pronoun and loyalty–friend-word links also weakened.
    - Some associations persisted: neuroticism–negative emotion, and purity–religion words.
  - Rewritten texts "tended to align with authors having traits commonly associated with older, male, politically liberal individuals," with "more positive moral valence with lower empathy."
  - Observationally, lexical and syntactic diversity in arXiv, Reddit, and Patch News fell after ChatGPT's launch.
- **Scope:** GPT-3.5, Llama 3 70B, and Gemini Pro. English. Published in *Nature Human Behaviour* in 2026.
- **Actionable rules:**
  - When revising someone's text, keep their characteristic markers: pronoun habits, emotion words (including negative ones), temporal focus, social words, sentence length, and rare and idiosyncratic words (hapaxes).
  - Do not raise positivity or soften negative affect.
  - Do not even out sentence complexity. If the author writes short and plain, or long and winding, keep that.
  - "Fix grammar" should mean fixing errors, not re-voicing the text.
- **Caveats:** Effects vary by model and prompt. Llama SG on arXiv increased variance, and Gemini on Reddit was non-significant.
- **Strength:** Strong (peer reviewed, multi-dataset). The observational part relies on a detector (Binoculars), which makes it weaker.

### 19. Homogenization in reviews and crowded fields
- [P6a] Higher estimated AI share correlates with more homogeneous review content. [P6b] LLM use is higher in crowded subfields. Rule: aim for content that only this author or reviewer would write, such as specific citations, disagreements, and local knowledge.

## E. Human-text features (what humans do that LLMs lack)

- **Agentless passives at natural rates** in news and academic prose [P1 §5].
- **Genre-specific registers.** Spoken and script text uses clausal coordination, fragments, and informal and obscene vocabulary. Certain obscenities appear over **100× less often** in LLM text [P1].
- **Fewer nominalizations and participial clauses.** Humans use more finite clauses with explicit subjects [P1].
- **Higher variance** in lexical diversity, hapax use, and syntactic depth across writers [P5]. Human style also carries identity: age, gender, emotion words, and pronoun habits [P5].
- **Citations and engagement** with specific prior work and with the interlocutor's argument (reviewers who replied to rebuttals and cited work showed less AI signal) [P6a].
- **Topic-specific vocabulary.** Human "excess words" follow real events, such as Covid-era nouns, rather than style verbs [P2].
- **Precise, unhedged verbs** ("amplified," not "seemed to amplify") and concrete, specific detail [P7].
- **Function-word profile.** Claude uses "the/and/to/of" far less than other models and humans [P4]. Rule: do not strip articles and connectives to sound terse.

## F. What does NOT work or backfires

- **Paraphrasing or translating** leaves the model fingerprint mostly intact (91–92% accuracy). Even summarization leaves well-above-chance signal [P4]. Synonym swapping alone fails.
- **Telling a model "no markdown"** removes headers and bullets but not em dashes. Explicit em-dash bans fail in some models [P11, weak]. Verify after drafting.
- **Token or word banning at scale** degrades quality: writing quality falls to 28/100 with 8k banned patterns, and "token banning becomes unusable at just 2,000" [P3]. A huge banlist is no substitute for good writing. Target the high-ratio items.
- **DPO-style suppression** cost 6–15 points of writing quality, while FTPO kept quality within about 1% [P3]. Analogy for a writer: replace the tell with the right word for the context, not with the nearest alternative.
- **Blanket "active voice" rules** move text away from human norms in genres where humans use agentless passives [P1].
- **Scale does not fix style.** Larger models were not more human-like [P1].
- **LLM self-detection of problem spans** is far below experts (precision about 0.44 vs 0.57) [P7]. Generic "make it less AI" self-edit prompts "do not work for long-form or paragraph-level writing" (cited in [P7]).
- **Fine-tuning on an author's corpus** removed detector signal (detection fell from 97% to 3%). Cliché density mediated 16.4% of expert dislike of prompted AI text, and only 1.3% after fine-tuning [P8, preprint]. The style tells are what experts penalize, and in-context prompting alone does not remove them.
- **Slop is partly subjective.** Binary "slop" labels had low inter-annotator agreement (κ −0.15 to 0.29). Slop judgments are best predicted by **relevance (β = 0.06), information density (0.05), and tone (0.05)**, while repetition and templatedness dominate in short QA [P9, preprint]. Word-level rules alone miss the main drivers: off-task content, low information per word, and an inappropriate tone.

## G. Statistical and other papers: what translates to writing

- [P2] and [P6a/b] are population-level estimators, not per-text detectors. What carries over to writing is the word lists and the finding that expanding notes into prose produces the signal while proofreading does not.
- [P3] is mostly a decoding/training method (Antislop sampler, FTPO). What carries over is the empirical slop lists and the "not X but Y" regex families above.
- [P8]: lay readers preferred prompted AI on quality (OR 1.82), while MFA experts strongly disliked it (OR 0.13). The penalty tracks detectable quirks such as cliché density. Write for the expert reader: removing clichés is what matters to skilled readers. Preprint, so moderate strength.
- [P9] gives an editorial checklist: density (substance per word), relevance to the prompt, tone fit, verbosity, word complexity (Gunning-Fog), templatedness (repeated POS structures), and repetition.

## Five-line summary
1. Instruction tuning, not model size, creates a dense noun-heavy register. GPT-4o uses present-participial clauses at 5.3× the human rate, nominalizations at 2.1×, and phrasal coordination at 1.9×, but agentless passives at half the human rate. Rewrite toward finite clauses and genre-appropriate register rather than mechanically purging passives [P1].
2. Excess vocabulary is mostly style verbs and evaluative adjectives: delves 28×, underscores 13.8×, showcasing 10.7×, meticulous 34.7×, tapestry 155×, camaraderie 162×, plus potential, crucial, comprehensive, notably, additionally. In fiction, add flicker, glint, gaze, murmured, "voice barely a whisper," and "air thick with" [P1, P2, P3, P6].
3. Structural tells include "not X, but Y" negative parallelism (6.3× in some models; four regex families), em dashes (medRxiv prevalence went from 4.2% to 20.3% by 2025), bolded list and header formatting (identifies the model with 73% accuracy from markdown alone), and assistant framing such as "Certainly," "Here is," and "let me know if" [P3, P4, P10].
4. The fingerprint survives paraphrase and translation (about 91%), so synonym swaps fail. Content selection, organization, density, relevance, and tone carry the signal, and professional writers mostly fix awkward phrasing, structure, redundant exposition, and clichés [P4, P7, P9].
5. LLM rewrites cut writing-complexity variance by 21–50% and erase identity markers (gender, age, emotion words) toward an "older, male, liberal, positive, low-empathy" voice. When revising, preserve the author's sentence-length range, rare words, pronoun and emotion habits, and negative affect, and fix errors without re-voicing [P5].
