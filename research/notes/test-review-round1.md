## T1: Lantern Noodle House blog post

**Human-likeness:** A (4). **Better:** A. It is cleaner and invents less, but it is about 170 words against a ~250 brief.

**AI tells, A**
- "had three problems on her desk": an announced count plus a stock metaphor. "A shorter menu helped with all three" then pays off the count too neatly.
- "We hear you.": a stock customer-service phrase whose only job is to sound warm.
- "Friday tickets now average 12 minutes, down from more than 25.": repeats a figure from two sentences earlier, as if the reader might have forgotten it.
- "If your usual order is still on the menu, it should reach the table faster now.": a soft closing line that wraps things up but adds little.
- Point of view drifts. The post says "we cut," then calls the owner "Dana Reyes, who owns the place," as if someone else were writing.

**AI tells, B**
- "It was not a small decision, so here is why we made it.": signposting.
- "The reasons were practical.": a throat-clearing topic sentence.
- "which is too long to ask anyone to wait for a bowl of noodles": a moralizing aside.
- "The kitchen is calmer, the food comes out hotter, and we are throwing away far less.": a tricolon of claimed results.
- "We know it cost us something.": a sentence that exists only to sound warm.
- "kindly but often": a cute paired-adverb flourish.
- "Thank you for sticking with us through the change.": a warm filler sign-off.
- "Come in on a Friday and see what a 12-minute ticket tastes like.": a slogan closer.

**Facts invented or changed**
- A: "We order fewer things, so less of it spoils" asserts that waste went down. The brief gives no figure after the change. "though we haven't set a date" is a harmless addition.
- B: three outcomes are invented: "calmer," "food comes out hotter," "throwing away far less." Also invented: the walk-in explanation, "kitchen was stretched thin," and the condition "when the kitchen has room for it." The Dana Reyes sign-off is fine.

**Anything lost**
- A is too short for a ~250-word post and leaves out any reason a reader should care beyond speed.
- B keeps every fact and fills the length, but it pads with invented results.

## T2: Email to landlord

**Human-likeness:** A (3). **Better:** A. It is firmer, which suits 58°F and a toddler, and it says how to reach the tenant. B's subject line is better because it puts the deadline in it.

**AI tells, A**
- "It's now Thursday. I called Monday to report it and left a voicemail on Tuesday. Nobody has called back.": four short declaratives in a row. The rhythm feels engineered to sound blunt.
- "so I can't wait on this much longer": fine, maybe slightly generic.
- Otherwise clean, with very few tells.

**AI tells, B**
- "so this is getting urgent for us": a softened intensifier.
- "so I know what to expect and can make sure someone is here to let them in": over-explains why he wants a name.
- "I'd appreciate a reply today, even if it's just to confirm a time.": a polite-ask template.
- "Thank you,": standard for a letter, not really a tell.

**Facts invented or changed**
- A: "roughly when" is a reasonable extension. "the number I called from" assumes nothing false.
- B: "I haven't heard back from either of you" invents two recipients. The brief has one landlord, so this reads like a template leftover. "a reply today" is an added demand.

**Anything lost**
- Neither version drops a fact. A doesn't say whose number to use beyond "the number I called from," which is fine.

## T3: Nurse and son, vending alcove, 3 a.m.

**Human-likeness:** B (3). **Better:** B. Its details are more specific (the bill trick, B4 stuck so try B5, Sacramento to Stockton, a burrito from a gas station), it gives a real backstory (the fight about the house), and it states less of the moral.

**AI tells, A**
- "hummed louder than it needed to": a familiar opening move.
- "from somewhere with a different time zone": a vague stand-in for a detail.
- "He laughed, and then it turned into something else": the stock laugh-becomes-sob move.
- "there were maybe three useful sentences for moments like this, and that most of the time the right thing was to stand there and be a person.": a wisdom statement. It is the story's moral, spoken by the narrator.
- "I wouldn't tell you that if it weren't true.": too-neat reassurance.
- "I think I was angry about that for thirty years." / "And now?" / "Now I'd give anything for him to turn it up.": a setup and payoff epiphany in tidy dialogue. This is the biggest tell in the piece.
- "Down the hall a monitor chirped twice and went quiet.": stock hospital ambience.
- "He followed her out of the alcove, the crackers still in his hand.": closes on an object image.

**AI tells, B**
- "She said it the way she must have said it a hundred times": a stock phrase.
- "I've heard it from three people. You're the first one who told me when you'd be back.": a writerly insight delivered as dialogue. It is too neat.
- "I don't even remember what the fight was about, only that I was right.": an aphorism that snaps shut.
- "She didn't tell him it was all right. He was grateful, and a little angry about being grateful.": names the emotion in a symmetrical construction.
- "Down the hall a monitor started chirping and someone silenced it.": the same stock ambience as A.
- "Then you'll have crackers.": a punchline reply set up for the laugh.
- "He laughed at that, which surprised him.": stock.
- "and then he stood up, and opened the crackers, and ate one ... before he went to room 12 with the rest.": polysyndeton for a literary close, again ending on the crackers.

**Shared template:** both versions use crackers from the machine, "comfortable," "I checked him twenty minutes ago," a monitor chirping down the hall, the son's childhood memory of his father, and the nurse walking him back to the room. Two stories this close suggest one underlying template, and a reader who sees both would notice.

**Facts:** no brief facts to violate. B has a small continuity question: "rounds on four" and "Room 12" read as 412, which is fine. The shoes-off detail and the clogs are consistent.

**Anything lost:** nothing. Both are at or slightly over ~400 words.

## T4: Fieldnote offline-mode LinkedIn post

**Human-likeness:** A (4). **Better:** A. It is clear, accurate and sounds like a real product person. B reads like a marketing template.

**AI tells, A**
- "Fieldnote now works offline." ... "Offline mode is in the app now.": the first and last lines echo each other, and the closing line is redundant with "available today."
- "out past the last tower": a slightly writerly phrase, though a good one.
- "that's the group we built it for": a mild audience-targeting formula.

**AI tells, B**
- "No more waiting until ..., and no more redoing paperwork in the truck.": the "No more X, no more Y" marketing formula.
- "That number was hard to ignore": filler.
- "where coverage is a luxury": a cliché.
- "substations, rights-of-way, basements, back roads": a four-item list of settings for color.
- "on real jobs, in real dead zones": "real ... real" repeated for emphasis.
- "their feedback shaped what you're getting today": stock beta language.
- "this one is for you": a stock closer.

**Facts invented or changed**
- A: none.
- B: the four locations, "redoing paperwork in the truck," "their feedback shaped" the product, and "Update the app and it's there" are all invented. They are plausible, but the brief doesn't say any of them.

**Anything lost**
- A has no call to action beyond "in the app now." It doesn't say what the beta proved. It is thin even for LinkedIn.

## T5: Nonprofit newsletter revision

**Human-likeness:** B (3). **Better:** A, by a small margin. A newsletter thanking volunteers can afford one warm sentence. B is so bare it reads curt.

**AI tells, A**
- "and what they did was remarkable": an evaluative filler that swaps one empty phrase ("a testament to") for another.
- "Thank you to every neighbor who showed up.": borderline. It keeps the original's "neighbors," which helps.
- "If you've been thinking about joining them, we'd love to have you.": a stock warm call to action.

**AI tells, B**
- "Thank you.": a one-word sentence, used as a deliberate minimalist beat.
- "get in touch": a generic call to action with no contact method. It reads like the original's "join us" with the flourish stripped off rather than rewritten.

**Facts:** both keep 214, more than 3,000 meals and 180 homebound seniors, and neither invents anything.

**Anything lost**
- B loses all sense of the community and of who is being thanked.
- Both drop any link between the new volunteers and the organization's mission. That link was empty in the original anyway.

## T6: Heat pump explainer

**Human-likeness:** A (3). **Better:** B, by a small margin. It is more accurate on the refrigerant cycle (evaporate and condense) and gives a concrete efficiency figure (about 3:1). A's closing advice about backup heat costs is the single most useful line in either version, though.

**AI tells, A**
- "Cold weather makes the job harder in two ways.": an announced count, and it is then broken, because frost becomes a third way.
- "Inside the outdoor unit, a refrigerant circulates in a loop. It runs through a coil outside": muddled. The loop runs between the two units, not inside the outdoor one.
- "That's why a heat pump can deliver more heat than the electricity it uses.": fine, a mild explainer formula.
- There are few stylistic tells otherwise.

**AI tells, B**
- "The process works like a refrigerator running in reverse.": a stock analogy, and slightly wrong. In heating mode a heat pump is simply a refrigerator, with the outdoors as the "inside."
- "Because the system moves heat instead of burning fuel to create it": a restatement of the opening paragraph.
- "The colder the outdoor air, the less heat ..., and the harder ...": a "the X-er, the Y-er" construction.
- "but the pattern holds: the colder it is, the less efficient they become.": a summary that restates the thesis, using a colon to set up a reveal.
- "Modern cold-climate models handle low temperatures far better than older ones": generic reassurance.

**Facts:** A's "air at 20°F" is fine. B's "three units of heat for every one unit of electricity" is typical but unsourced, and it hedges with "Often."

**Anything lost**
- A never puts a number on efficiency, so "less efficient" stays abstract.
- B runs about 300 words, over the ~250 target, and drops the practical point about cost.

## Overall verdict

The terse, mostly-A style reads more human in 4 of 6 tasks and is better writing in 4 of 6. It invents very little. Its tells are structural rather than lexical: announced counts, closing lines that echo the opening, and a habit of cutting until the piece is under length and cold.

The fuller, mostly-B style keeps showing the classic tells. It invents outcomes and color, uses tricolons, the "No more X" formula, warm filler and slogan closers. Its one clear win, the T3 story, came from concrete, odd details, not from its prose habits.

Both sets clearly come from the same story template in T3, which is a fingerprint a reader could spot.

## Five patterns for the better (A) set to fix next

1. **Moral and epiphany lines in fiction.** Examples: "the right thing was to stand there and be a person" and "Now I'd give anything for him to turn it up." Cut the line that states the meaning, and let the action carry it.
2. **Announced counts.** Examples: "three problems on her desk" / "helped with all three" and "harder in two ways" (followed by a third). Just list the things.
3. **Bookend and wrap-up closers.** Examples: "Offline mode is in the app now," "If your usual order is still on the menu, it should reach the table faster now," and "the crackers still in his hand." End on the last useful fact, or on a specific action, and don't circle back to the opening.
4. **Under-delivery passed off as restraint.** T1 is about 70% of the requested length, and T4 and T5 lose their calls to action and their sense of who the reader is. Spare doesn't have to mean thin. Fill the space with brief facts, worked through in more depth, not with invented color.
5. **Warm-filler swaps and staccato rhythm.** Examples: "We hear you," "what they did was remarkable," "we'd love to have you," and runs of four short declaratives in a row (T2, T4). Swapping "a testament to" for "remarkable" is the same move in different words. Vary sentence length on purpose, and make warmth come from a specific detail rather than a stock phrase.

Also: stop reusing hospital stock details in stories ("checked him twenty minutes ago," a monitor chirping down the hall). Both versions use them, so they read as defaults.
