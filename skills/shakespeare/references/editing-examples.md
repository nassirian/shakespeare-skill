# Editing examples

I wrote these examples for teaching; none are quoted from the studies. In each one, the facts stated are all the source material there is. The revisions show choices you can make, not templates to copy.

## Replace empty framing with the event

**Source facts:** The library will open on Sundays starting October 4. Hours are noon to four. Volunteers will staff the desk.

**Before:** In today's ever-changing world, libraries play a crucial role in fostering vibrant communities. This exciting new chapter is a testament to the power of collaboration.

**After:** Starting October 4, the library will open on Sundays from noon to four, with volunteers staffing the desk.

**Why:** The reader learns what changes and when. Adding a claim about demand, visitor numbers or funding would need a source.

## Cut the participle tail and the noun stack

**Before:** The council approved the new parking rules on Tuesday, highlighting its commitment to the revitalization of the downtown area and underscoring the importance of accessibility for residents.

**After:** The council approved the new parking rules on Tuesday.

**Why:** The two -ing tails added significance the source doesn't support. If the source gives the council's actual reason, put it in its own sentence with its own subject: "The mayor said the old two-hour limit was driving shoppers to the mall."

## Drop the straw contrast

**Before:** This isn't just a software update. It's a complete rethinking of how teams collaborate.

**After:** The update adds shared drafts and comment threads, so a team can edit one document instead of emailing versions around.

**Why:** Nobody claimed it was "just" an update. The revision says what changed.

## Preserve uncertainty while simplifying

**Before:** It is important to note that the pilot may reduce processing time by approximately 12%, although this estimate is based on only 18 cases and has not yet been independently verified.

**After:** The pilot may cut processing time by about 12%. That figure comes from just 18 cases and hasn't been independently checked.

**Why:** The estimate, the sample size and the qualification all survive. "The pilot cuts processing time by 12%" would change the claim.

## Keep the author's voice: a light edit

**Source:** "I liked the first half. Then the book got so busy explaining itself that I stopped caring."

**Light edit:** I liked the first half. Then the book got so busy explaining itself that I stopped caring.

**Why:** An edit can leave a passage unchanged. "The latter portion undermines its narrative momentum through excessive exposition" would swap the author's voice for a generic one.

## Don't polish a review into a verdict template

**Source:** "ok the wait was brutal and the host was kind of snooty but honestly?? best ramen I've had in this city. go."

**Bad revision:** Despite a long wait and somewhat unwelcoming service, this restaurant serves some of the best ramen in the city. Highly recommended.

**Good revision (if asked to clean up for posting):** OK, the wait was brutal and the host was a bit snooty. But honestly? Best ramen I've had in this city. Go.

**Why:** The bad version merges the complaints into one balanced "Despite" sentence and drops the author's voice. The good version fixes capitals and punctuation and keeps everything else.

## Don't upgrade plain verbs

**Before (author's draft):** We used a smaller model because the big one needed too much memory. It still worked well on short inputs.

**Over-polished:** We employed a more compact model, as the larger variant demanded substantial computational resources. Notably, it demonstrated robust performance on shorter inputs.

**Keep:** the original. If anything is unclear, fix that one thing: "It still worked well on inputs under 512 tokens" if that's the real limit.

## Keep the notes' density

**User's notes:** Q3 revenue up 8%. Churn flat. Hiring freeze through Dec. Two enterprise deals slipped to Q4.

**Inflated:** This quarter, we're excited to share that revenue grew by a solid 8%, reflecting the continued strength of our offering. Customer retention remained stable, underscoring the loyalty of our user base. To ensure sustainable growth, we've made the strategic decision to pause hiring through December. Additionally, two enterprise deals have shifted into Q4, positioning us for a strong finish to the year.

**Better:** Revenue rose 8% in Q3 and churn was flat. Two enterprise deals we expected this quarter slipped to Q4. We're freezing hiring through December.

**Why:** The notes held four facts. The inflated version adds spin ("positioning us for a strong finish") that the notes don't support. Putting the slipped deals before the freeze is a judgment call the author can reverse.

## End when the content ends

**Before (last paragraph of a how-to):** And there you have it! With these simple steps, you'll be well on your way to a perfectly seasoned cast-iron pan. Remember, the journey of cooking is all about patience and practice. Happy cooking!

**After:** Delete the paragraph. The previous one ended on the last step: "Let it cool in the oven before you put it away."

## Match emotional weight

**Source facts:** A delivery is two days late. The replacement is due Friday. The sender is apologizing.

**Before:** While this journey has presented challenges, we remain excited about the opportunity to turn this setback into a meaningful step forward.

**After:** I'm sorry your delivery is two days late. The replacement is due Friday.

**Why:** An apology plus the useful information fits the situation. Don't promise Friday delivery if the source says only that it is due then.

## Don't invent a story for specificity

**Request:** "Write a personal story about how our software saved a client ten hours."

**Available material:** No client story, no measurement, no permission to invent.

**Right response:** Ask for the real client example and what the ten-hour figure measures. If the user wants a template, mark the missing details as placeholders. Never present an invented testimonial as real.

## Keep technical precision

**Before:** The trial demonstrated a statistically significant association, but its observational design does not establish causation.

**Decision:** Resolve the conflict between "trial" and "observational design" with the author or the source before polishing. Keep the distinction between association and causation.

## Fiction: name the feeling sometimes

**Before:** Her chest tightened. A cold knot formed in her stomach as the scent of rain and old paper filled the room, and she let out a breath she didn't know she'd been holding.

**After:** She was frightened, and it made her rude. "You could have called," she said.

**Why:** Three stock body sensations and a smell opener became a plain emotion and an action that shows what the fear does to her.

## Fiction: don't state the lesson

**Before (final lines):** As she watched the sun set over the harbor, Mara finally understood that letting go of her father's boat didn't mean letting go of him. Some things, she realized, you carry with you.

**After:** She signed the papers on the hood of the buyer's truck. He asked if she wanted the old compass. She said no, then went back for it.

**Why:** The reader can see that she hasn't let go. The ending stops on an action and leaves the meaning implied.

## Keep a useful list

**Request:** "Make these instructions clear. Keep the three steps."

**Before:** In order to successfully complete the process, it is crucial to first save your work, then proceed to close the application, and finally restart the device.

**After:**

1. Save your work.
2. Close the application.
3. Restart the device.

**Why:** The list makes the sequence usable and honors the request. A real three-step process is not a tell.
